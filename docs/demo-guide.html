Kerbside · Demo guide

Showing it to your professor

How to prepare the iPad, what to tap, and what to say. The whole demo takes about seven minutes.

The one thing to know. There is no server. When the page opens it downloads Python and runs the real pipeline on the iPad itself. So the first load needs internet and takes a minute or so, and the iPad has to stay on the page while it loads: leaving it starts the download again.

The day before · 15 minutesSet up the iPad

  1. Check the site is live On the Vercel dashboard, the latest deployment should say Ready. Copy its address (it ends in .vercel.app).
  2. Open it once on the iPad, on good Wi-Fi Open the address in Safari. Wait for the loading screen to tick through all five steps and the console to appear. The first visit downloads about 35 MB, and after that it is stored on the iPad. Note how many seconds loading took. That is roughly what you will wait on the day.
  3. Put it on the Home Screen This makes it open full-screen like an app, without Safari's address bar. Tap the Share button ⬆︎ → Add to Home Screen → Add. A Kerbside icon appears.
  4. Rehearse once Open it from the new icon and run through the demo below, top to bottom.
  5. Stop the screen sleeping Settings → Display & Brightness → Auto-Lock → Never. Turn on a Focus / Do Not Disturb so no notification covers the screen. Charge the iPad.
  6. If you can, test on the network you'll present on The page downloads Python from cdn.jsdelivr.net. Some university networks block unusual sites. If it will not load there, a phone hotspot works.

Five minutes beforeGet it ready

  1. Close other apps and Safari tabs The pipeline needs memory. Closing everything else is the best insurance against the "could not start" message.
  2. Open Kerbside from the Home Screen icon Wait for the console to appear. Don't switch apps while it loads, and don't tap the overview link on the loading screen: from the Home Screen icon it opens in Safari, which counts as switching apps.
  3. Hold the iPad sideways (landscape) Everything fits on one screen: the menu on the left, the video in the middle, and the list of vehicles on the right. Held upright, the vehicle list moves behind a Vehicles button in the bottom-right corner.

The demo · about 7 minutesWhat to tap and what to say

  1. Introduce it 30 s "Kerbside measures traffic from one fixed camera: how many vehicles, how fast in km/h, which lane, and what size. It uses only classical image processing, with no machine learning anywhere." Point at the running in your browser label at the bottom of the screen. "There's no server here. The actual Python code is running on this iPad right now."
  2. Play the video 1 min Tap Play. "Each box is a tracked vehicle, labelled with its ID, speed, size class and colour. The dot at the bottom of each box is the point where the tyres meet the road. That's the point I measure speed from, because it's the only part of the vehicle that's actually on the road surface. The dashed line is the counting line, and the counts per lane go up as vehicles cross it." When it stops, tap a vehicle in the list on the right. "Every vehicle's speed is measured two independent ways, from its position and from how its texture moves. Comparing them is how I found where the errors come from."
  3. Tap a pixel 30 s Tap on any car in the video. "This shows what the pipeline decided about that exact pixel: its colour, what the empty road looks like at that spot, the difference, and whether that crossed the threshold. Every decision can be checked like this."
  4. Show inside the pipeline 1½ min Tap Pipeline in the left menu. Scroll down slowly. "These are the nine stages, each showing its actual output for this frame. First the background model: what the empty road looks like. Then everything that differs from it. Then shadows removed by colour. Then the shapes separated into individual vehicles. The small tags like U2 and U6 are the syllabus units each stage uses."
  5. Measure a real distance 1 min Tap Calibration → Measure a distance, then tap two points on the road on either side of one lane. "Four clicks on the road give an exact mapping from pixels to metres. That's what turns pixels into real distances and speeds. A lane should come out close to 3.5 metres, which is the width the scene was built with." Tap Bird's-eye. "Here's the same road seen from directly above using that mapping. The lane lines come out parallel, which is how you can see the calibration is right."
  6. Show the results 1 min Tap Analytics. "Counts per lane and per size class, the speed distribution, and the 85th-percentile speed, which is the figure road engineers use. Anything over the limit is only 'flagged for review'. It's a measuring tool, not a speed camera, and it never calls anything a violation."
  7. Change a setting live 1–2 min, optional Tap Console. In the left menu tap S4, then change Threshold from otsu to fixed. A panel appears: "Running the pipeline in your browser". It takes about half a minute on a laptop and longer on an iPad (you'll know how long from your rehearsal). Talk through it: "Changing one setting re-runs the whole pipeline on every frame, because each frame depends on the ones before it. It's all happening on the iPad, which is why it takes a moment." When it finishes, the boxes and counts change. Then tap Reset stage to put the setting back.
  8. Close honestly 30 s "I set eight measurable targets before writing any code. Five were met, three weren't, and I haven't changed any of them afterwards. The three misses share one cause: the detection step sometimes splits one vehicle into two pieces. I proved it by re-running the scene with shadows switched off entirely, and accuracy barely changed. The speed method that doesn't depend on the vehicle's outline is twice as accurate, which confirms it."

If something goes wrongQuick fixes

  • Stuck on the loading screen: check the Wi-Fi, then reload the page. The very first load needs internet.
  • "The pipeline could not start": close other apps and tabs, then reload. It needs a recent iPad and about 1 GB of free memory.
  • It feels slow: that's expected. The processing is running on the iPad's own processor. Keep talking; the progress panel shows it's working.
  • The screen went dark or you switched apps: the page may restart loading. Wait for it, then carry on.
  • Nothing works on the day: open the one-page overview (below) and walk through it instead. It loads instantly. Or run the full version on a laptop with .venv/Scripts/python.exe run_kerbside.py --scenario free_flow --open.

Be ready forQuestions they may ask

Why not just use YOLO or another neural network?
It would detect better, but it would answer a different question. This course is classical image processing, and here the geometry does the detector's job: a vehicle is classified by its size in metres, not by how it looks.
Why are the accuracy figures below target?
One named cause: the detection step splits a vehicle into two pieces, for example the roof and the bumper. Counts, sizes and one of the speed methods all miss for that same reason. The fix belongs in the segmentation, and tuning the later stages barely changed anything, which is how I know.
How is the speed actually worked out?
The calibration maps pixels to metres on the road. The point where the vehicle meets the road is tracked from frame to frame, and speed is distance travelled divided by time. A second, independent method matches the vehicle's texture between frames and does the same.
Could it be used as a speed camera?
No. A legal speed device needs type approval and certified calibration, which this doesn't have. It measures, it never labels anything a violation, and it never reads number plates or faces.
Is this running live, or is it a recording?
Live. Change a setting and it re-runs the whole pipeline on the iPad. The traffic itself is generated by a built-in simulator, which is what makes exact accuracy figures possible: every true speed is known.

1Opening: what it is1 min

My project is Kerbside, a traffic analytics system. It takes video from one fixed camera beside a road and, for every vehicle that passes, it measures five things: how many vehicles there are, each one's speed in kilometres per hour, which lane it's in, its size class, and its colour.

The defining rule is that it uses only classical digital image processing: filtering, thresholding, morphology, colour models, frequency-domain methods, motion estimation and geometry. There is no neural network, no pretrained model and no machine learning anywhere in the pipeline. That isn't a limitation I worked around; it's the question the project asks: can classical methods produce a calibrated physical measurement, not just a detection?

2Why it's hard1 min

Every one of those outputs is a measurement in metres and seconds, but the image is indexed in pixels and frames. Five things make that difficult.

First, a pixel isn't a fixed distance: near the camera a metre might be thirty pixels, far away it's nine, so every size-based rule has to change with the row of the image. Second, cast shadows move with the vehicle, so every motion test sees them as foreground, and they join neighbouring cars into one blob. Third, a vehicle's dark windows can split it into a roof and a bumper. Fourth, video files often lie about their frame rate, and speed divides by time. And fifth, vehicles hide behind each other.

3The core idea: geometry instead of training1½ min

A learned detector answers "is this a car?" from appearance. I answer it from size in real-world metres, and that single substitution is what removes the need for any trained model.

The road is a flat plane, and a camera maps one plane to another through a homography, a 3×3 matrix with eight degrees of freedom. You recover it from four point correspondences: the four corners of a rectangle of known size on the road, such as one lane wide and one road marking long. I implemented the estimation from scratch using the normalised Direct Linear Transform. The normalisation matters, because without it the equations are badly conditioned exactly where the perspective is strongest.

Once calibrated, the bottom-centre of each vehicle's box, where the tyres meet the road, has a position in metres. Speed is distance over time, and a 4.5-metre footprint is a car while an 11-metre one is a bus. The decision boundary is a number anyone can check.

4Building the world: exact ground truth1 min

You can't report a speed error without knowing the true speed, and hand-labelled video gives you boxes, not velocities. So I wrote a synthetic traffic renderer that knows every answer exactly: every vehicle's position, speed, lane, class and visible area, in every frame.

The vehicles follow a car-following model, so traffic behaves realistically. The renderer handles occlusion properly and draws physically correct shadows, and there are eight scenarios, each built to test one failure mode: free flow, congestion, night, rain, heavy occlusion, camera shake, long shadows and motion blur. I verified the renderer's own geometry to under a millionth of a pixel, because if the ground truth were wrong, every error figure would be measuring the renderer instead of the pipeline.

5The pipeline, stage by stage3 min

The pipeline has nine stages. Every one carries a written rationale and its syllabus unit, and the interface shows both next to its controls.

S1, Ingest, decodes the video and measures the true frame rate from timestamps rather than trusting the file. S2, Stabilise, removes camera shake by phase correlation in the frequency domain, refined to sub-pixel accuracy.

S3, Background, learns the empty road with a running average and a temporal median, both written by hand. The running average is literally a temporal low-pass filter: the Unit 2 smoothing operator with time as its axis.

S4, Foreground, finds what differs from the background using Otsu thresholding, then cleans the mask with morphology whose element size is worked out from the homography for each row of the image. S5, Shadow, removes cast shadows with a test in HSV colour space: a shadow keeps its hue and loses brightness by a bounded ratio.

S6, Blobs, turns the mask into separate vehicles with connected components, rejects shapes of the wrong real-world size, splits merged vehicles with a watershed, and joins fragments that fit inside one vehicle's outline. S7, Track, follows each vehicle in world metres, so a two-metre gate means two metres everywhere in the image. S8, Speed, measures speed two ways, which I'll come back to. And S9, Classify, assigns the size class from the footprint in metres and names the colour.

6Measuring speed two independent ways1½ min

Speed is measured two ways for every vehicle, and they're designed to fail for different reasons, which is what makes comparing them informative.

Route A tracks the point where the vehicle meets the road through the homography. That point is actually the vehicle's front edge, half a length short of its centre, so the position is biased. But the bias is constant, so it cancels when two positions are subtracted. That gives an unbiased speed from a biased position.

Route B uses block matching: it follows the vehicle's texture from frame to frame. That texture sits about a metre above the road, so I map its motion through the homography for a plane at that height, not the road plane. Using the road plane would leave an error that never averages away.

I deliberately don't use the blob's centre point. It isn't on the road at all, and its error grows with the vehicle's height, which never looks like a bug. It just looks like trucks being slower than cars.

7What I found along the way2 min

Some of the most useful results came from textbook methods failing on their own assumptions. Four examples.

Greyscale can't see a red car. Converting to grey weights red at only 0.299, so on my own palette a red car differs from the asphalt by 1.2 grey levels, but by 94 in its red channel. Four of ten body colours were essentially invisible. The difference is now taken across colour channels.

Hue is meaningless on grey asphalt. Hue is undefined when saturation is near zero, and the textbook hue test rejected half of a correctly found shadow. It now only applies above a saturation floor.

A closing element must be as tall as a vehicle, not as long as the road. A metre of height is about 25 pixels at mid-image, while a metre along the road is about 4. Sized the wrong way the element was two pixels tall, and every vehicle came out as two blobs.

Sub-pixel phase correlation needs more than a parabola. The standard three-point parabolic fit lands about a pixel out, and OpenCV's own function has the same problem. Refining on an upsampled Fourier grid brings it under a tenth of a pixel.

9The diagnosis: one cause behind the misses1 min

The three misses aren't three separate problems. The detection step sometimes splits one vehicle into two pieces, and that one defect lowers the count, the size class and the Route A speed together.

I didn't assume that; I tested it. Shadow removal works: it removes 87 to 97 percent of true shadow pixels. The decisive experiment was rendering the exact same scene with no shadows at all. Frame accuracy barely changed. If shadows were the problem, removing them perfectly would have fixed it, and it didn't.

The two speed routes independently agree. Route A depends on the edge of the vehicle's mask and reads on average 14 km/h too slow; Route B ignores the outline and is much more accurate. Two methods built to fail differently point to the same defect, which is what turns a number into a diagnosis.

10The system around it1 min

The pipeline sits inside a complete application: a web console with the live video, an inspector for each vehicle, a view of every stage's output, labs for shadows and motion, analytics, nine comparison studies and a one-click report.

It also runs entirely in the browser. The real Python code is compiled to WebAssembly and runs on the viewer's own device, with no server, which is how it is hosted online. It's about four and a half times slower there, so it uses a smaller frame.

It's backed by 289 automated tests. Two of the rules are enforced by tests rather than just stated: one checks that no machine-learning library is imported anywhere, and another runs the whole pipeline twice and requires identical results. The one exception, OpenCV's standard background subtractor, is kept only as a labelled baseline to measure my own models against, and it's never the default.

11Limitations and ethics45 s

It assumes a fixed camera and a flat road, since the geometry depends on both. It's too slow for live video at present. And the accuracy targets aren't met, for the reason I've explained.

Ethically, it measures speed but it is not a speed camera. A legal speed device needs type approval and certified calibration, which this doesn't have. Anything over the limit is only "flagged for review", never a violation, and it never reads number plates or recognises faces.

12Close30 s

To summarise: geometry did a detector's job. A calibrated homography turned classification into arithmetic on metres and turned a tracked blob into a physical speed. Textbook methods were tested rather than assumed, and several failed on their own terms. And where I missed a target, I measured why instead of moving the target.

Thank you. I'm happy to go into any stage, or change something live so you can see it respond.

?Questions they may ask

Why not just use YOLO or another neural network?
It would detect better and answer a different question. The course is classical image processing, and here the geometry does the detector's job. The trade-off is stated, not hidden.
Why is the accuracy so far below the targets?
One named cause: segmentation splits vehicles into pieces. A parameter sweep on the speed stage moved its error by only 0.2 km/h, which shows the fix belongs earlier, in segmentation, not in tuning.
Did you check your own background models against a standard one?
Yes, against OpenCV's MOG2 as a labelled baseline. MOG2 had the best recall and precision per vehicle, but it counted 14 vehicles where there were 9, because it fragments vehicles more. The target is a count, so it lost on the measure that mattered. My temporal median beat my own default too, 0.367 against 0.250, but that was on one clip, so I recorded it rather than changing the default.
How do you know the synthetic results mean anything?
Because the geometry of the renderer and the pipeline were verified against each other to under a millionth of a pixel, and each scenario isolates one real failure mode. Synthetic and real results are never averaged together.
What would you do next?
Fix the fragmentation at the foreground stage, with a colour-consistency region grow seeded from the mask, then speed up block matching, which is most of the frame time.

Image and Video Processing · accuracy record

What Kerbside measures, and how well

Every number below is measured against exact ground truth, compared against a target that was written before the code existed, and reported with its verdict — including the three targets the system does not meet.

Where these numbers come from. Vehicles are rendered by a synthetic camera model that records, per frame and per vehicle, exactly where each one was, how fast it was going, in which lane and of what size. Accuracy is therefore the difference between what the pipeline reports and what the renderer knows — not a hand annotation, and not an estimate. Every figure is asserted by a test in tests/, and the clips regenerate byte-identically from their seed, so each one is reproducible from a clean checkout.

The headline

Eight gates, five met

Each build phase had one numeric criterion, fixed in writing before any of its code was written. A criterion chosen after the measurement is not a criterion, it is a description — so none of these was adjusted afterwards, and the three failures are reported as failures.

What this says

The system is accurate where the problem is geometric and inaccurate where the problem is photometric. Everything that depends on the camera model — projection, calibration, the homography, the motion vectors — lands within a fraction of a percent of truth. Everything that depends on deciding which pixels belong to a vehicle lands well short.

The three missed gates are not three separate defects. Phases 3 and 4 are the same defect measured one stage apart, and Phase 5 is a cost, not an error.

What this says

The failure is fragmentation, not blindness. 83% of vehicles are found; the count is wrong because a single vehicle frequently arrives as two blobs. Split events outnumber merge events 87 to 40, and a vehicle reported as two pieces fails a count comparison exactly as surely as one that was never found at all.

Two fixes were made and measured. Correcting the closing element's height (see Why it fails) accounted for most of the original error, and a geometric fragment merge in S6 — which asks the homography how tall a vehicle standing at that point on the road would appear, and merges pieces that fit inside one plausible silhouette — halved split events from 144 to 87 and lifted recall from 0.80 to 0.83.

Reaching 98% frame agreement would need recall and precision both above 0.99, roughly an order of magnitude better than 0.83. The honest reading: the mask is good enough to track from, and not yet good enough to count from frame by frame.

Shadow removal is not the constraint, and this was tested rather than assumed: suppression removes 87–97% of true shadow pixels per frame, turning it off does not improve the count, and re-rendering the clip with no shadows at all leaves frame agreement essentially unchanged.

What this says

The two routes agreeing on one defect is the strongest single result in the project. They were built to fail independently: Route A differences the ground point of the bounding box through the road homography, and Route B tiles the vehicle, discards untextured blocks, matches the rest against the previous frame, takes the median displacement, refines it to sub-pixel by phase correlation and maps it through the homography of the plane at the height the blocks sit at.

Route A's position is defined by the edge of the mask, and that mask under-covers the vehicle rear. A short mask shortens the measured displacement, which under-reads the speed — and the measured bias is −14.35 km/h, reading slow, exactly as that explanation predicts. Route B never touches the mask boundary and its bias is a quarter of that. Two methods that share no failure mode pointing at the same cause is evidence that segmentation, not measurement, is the binding constraint.

Route B's remaining error is structural, and this was tested rather than asserted: sweeping the smoothing window and the gain moved its MAE between 9.4 and 9.6 km/h — essentially flat. There is nothing left to tune in S8. The fix lives in S4–S6, where the mask is made. Tuning until the number looked better would have hidden that.

Class accuracy at 52% has the same single cause. Classification is by real-world footprint in metres, which is a sound basis — but a mask that splits a vehicle halves its footprint, so the class is wrong for precisely the vehicles the mask fragments.

A third option, the blob centroid, is rejected on geometry rather than on measurement: a centroid sits in the middle of the visible silhouette, which is not on the calibrated road plane at all, and its error grows with distance and with vehicle height — correlation ≈ 0.85 with height. It never presents as a bug; it presents as trucks reading differently from cars. It is computed, and used for association only.

What this says

One stage dominates. S8 searches every textured block inside every vehicle box, which costs roughly two-thirds of the frame budget on its own and takes the pipeline from 4.0 fps to 1.1. Both numbers are reported — quoting only the 4.0 would describe a pipeline that detects vehicles but measures nothing about them.

Four optimisations during Phase 3 tripled the detection rate: replacing np.minimum.at over a flattened label image (23 ms per call, three calls per frame) with one C pass; replacing hole filling by iterated geodesic reconstruction — which needs as many full-frame dilations as the longest path inside a hole — with a single flood fill from outside; slicing each component's bounding box instead of comparing over the whole frame; and float32 rather than float64 throughout the mask stages, because the input is 8-bit.

The browser figure matters for the demonstration rather than for the accuracy: clip size was chosen by measuring in a real browser, not by scaling the native timing. At 480×270 the pipeline counts nothing at all — vehicles are too small at the counting line to become confirmed tracks — while 560×315 counts five and beats 640×360 on both count and time.

What this says

Calibration accuracy is bounded by click precision, not by the estimator. The DLT recovers the rendering homography to one part in 109 from exact input; every percent lost after that is the user's hand. That is why the calibration view offers zoom and recommends a large quadrilateral, and why the accuracy claim is stated together with the range it holds over — past about 60 m a single pixel is worth metres.

The last row is the important one for anyone repeating this: the 2% criterion is resolution-dependent. The identical half-pixel jitter that gives 0.14% at 720p gives 2.05% at 320×180 and misses. The criterion has to be measured at the resolution the user actually clicks on.

One negative result is worth as much as the positive ones. A four-point calibration's reprojection residual is meaningless: four points determine a homography exactly, so the residual is zero by construction no matter what was clicked or what real-world dimensions were typed in. A self-consistent calibration can still be wrong by a constant factor, and every speed would be wrong with it. Only the two-point distance check catches that.

What this says

MOG2 has the best recall and the best precision and is not the best model for this job. It finds 95.1% of vehicles and then reports 14 vehicles where 9 passed, because a per-pixel Gaussian mixture fragments a single vehicle more readily than a median over time does. Recall and precision are per-vehicle measures; the gate is a count. The model that wins the first two loses the one that was set in advance. Reading a per-vehicle metric as though it were the gate is the mistake this table exists to prevent.

The three-frame difference shows the same trap from the other side: precision 1.000, and it is the worst model here. It only ever fires on a moving edge, so nearly everything it reports is real and nearly everything real is missed.

The temporal median beats the shipped default on both stated criteria — frame agreement 0.367 against 0.250 and count accuracy 0.889 against 0.778 — at roughly half the throughput. The default has not been changed, and deliberately so: this is one 8-second clip, and changing a default on one clip is fitting the default to the clip. A 2.5-second clip put the same figure at 0.809, which is the reminder that a short clip is a different experiment and not a cheap version of a long one.

What this says

The block-matching rows are the pair worth reading twice. The fast searches are 30-odd times cheaper and still predict the next frame at 35 dB, which is entirely adequate for a video codec — and they land five pixels from the true vector, which is not adequate for a speed measurement. The same algorithm is a good choice for one job and a bad choice for the other. That distinction is the finding, not a caveat.

The Canny figure of 0.86 against OpenCV is the honest number on a real frame, and the 0.97 on a synthetic diagonal shows the implementation is right where the ground truth is unambiguous. It was 0.40 until a sign error was found: in image coordinates rows increase downward, so a +45° gradient points down-right, and non-maximum suppression for that sector must compare (+1, +1) against (−1, −1). With the diagonal sectors the wrong way round the algorithm silently deletes diagonal edges — no crash, no warning, 0.86 → 0.40. Comparing against a reference implementation is what caught it.

Two more corrections came out of measuring rather than assuming. Sub-pixel phase correlation needs more than a parabolic fit: for a fractional shift the inverse transform of a pure phase ramp is a periodic sinc with alternating side lobes, so a three-point parabola lands about a pixel out — and OpenCV's own phaseCorrelate has the same problem, measured. Refining on an upsampled DFT grid instead brings it under a tenth of a pixel. And Otsu's criterion is flat across an empty histogram gap, so a plain argmax returns the lowest level in the gap, hard against the upper tail of the dark mode, admitting far more noise than the middle would; this implementation takes the plateau midpoint, which is no less optimal by Otsu's own criterion and markedly more stable.

One study reports a difference that cannot exist, and says so. m-connectivity and 8-connectivity always induce identical components — provably, and asserted over random masks. What m changes is the uniqueness of the path between two pixels, which matters for boundary following and thinning, not for counting. The connectivity study therefore reports a count difference for 4 against the other two, and path ambiguity for m. Reporting a count difference for m would be reporting something that cannot happen.

The diagnosis

Every missed accuracy target traces to one place

The count, the class, the lane and the Route A speed bias are not four problems. They are one problem — the mask — observed at four different stages, and the measurements below are what establish that rather than assert it.

  1. A greyscale difference cannot see a red vehicle BT.601 weights red at 0.299, so against this project's own asphalt a red body differs from the road by 1.2 grey levels — and by 94 in its red channel. Blue, brown and green are within 10 levels too: four of ten body colours are not merely hard to detect but invisible. The mask then contains their shadow and not them, which looks like a shadow problem and is a colour problem introduced two stages earlier. The default difference is therefore taken across colour channels; greyscale is kept only as the baseline that demonstrates the failure.
  2. Hue is undefined where saturation is near zero, and asphalt is neutral At a background saturation of about 0.075, the hue difference across foreground pixels has a median of 10 and a 95th percentile of 67. That is not a measurement of colour, it is the arctangent of two small noisy differences. Applying the textbook 10-unit hue tolerance there rejects half of a correctly identified shadow, so the hue condition is enforced only above a saturation floor.
  3. The closing element was six times too short A silhouette extends upward from its ground point, so a metre of height projects at close to the across-road scale — about 25 px/m at image row 300 — while a metre along the road projects at about 4 px/m. Scaled by the wrong axis the element comes out two pixels tall, the dark window band across a vehicle survives as a gap, and every vehicle is reported as two blobs, a roof and a bumper. It presents as a tracking problem. It is a structuring element sized against the wrong scale.
  4. Fragmentation is what the count actually measures 87 split events against 40 merges, down from 144 splits once S6 gained a geometric fragment merge. Frame agreement 25.0%, mean absolute count error 1.62 vehicles per frame.
  5. One stage later, the same mask sets the class and the count Classification is by footprint in metres, and a split mask halves the footprint: class 52%. Counting: 90.9%, twelve where eleven passed.
  6. And one stage after that, it sets the speed bias A mask that under-covers the vehicle rear shortens the differenced displacement, so Route A under-reads: bias −14.35 km/h. Route B, which never touches the mask boundary, biases −3.88. A tuning sweep moves Route B by 0.2 km/h and no further.

What this says

Improving S8 cannot help, and the flat tuning sweep is the proof. The accuracy ceiling of the whole system is set in S3–S6, where pixels are decided to be vehicle or road — and the interventions that moved the numbers so far were all of that kind: colour differencing instead of luma, a saturation-gated hue test, a correctly scaled structuring element, an area-bounded hole fill, and a geometric fragment merge.

Two further findings in the same family are worth naming because each looked like a different bug. Filling holes after removing a shadow restores the shadow, because subtracting a shadow from inside a larger foreground region leaves an enclosed hole that an unbounded fill puts straight back — bounding the fill by area separates the two cases cleanly, since a windscreen is hundreds of pixels and a cast shadow is tens of thousands. And the selective background update was keyed on a foreground mask that is rebuilt per frame and is therefore always empty when S3 runs, so the feature silently never fired until the previous frame's mask was handed to it explicitly.

Method

How to read these numbers

Four of these were mistakes in the evaluation itself, found and corrected. They are listed because a wrong metric costs more than a wrong algorithm: it hides the algorithm.

  • Frame agreement is an exacting measure, and deliberately so. It asks that every frame carry precisely the right count. At a mean absolute error of 1.62 vehicles per frame, 25% is not close to 98% and no reading of the number makes it closer.
  • Recall counts vehicles found, not blob–vehicle pairs. A vehicle reported as two fragments has been found once. Crediting it twice let recall exceed 1.0, which is how the error surfaced.
  • The visibility gate says what must be found, not what may be found. Gating ground truth by range while scoring every blob counts a correct detection just outside the range as a false positive — worst exactly where it matters least, since a one-pixel ground-point error at 100 m is about eight metres.
  • A per-vehicle metric is not the gate. Recall and precision describe vehicles; the count describes a total. MOG2 wins the first two and loses the third, and the background study exists to make that distinction visible.
  • One clip is one experiment. The background comparison is an 8-second free-flow clip. A 2.5-second clip moved frame agreement for the same model from 0.367 to 0.809. A short clip is a different experiment, not a cheap version of a long one — which is why no default was changed on the strength of it.
  • Two figures are reported for throughput, not the flattering one. 4.0 fps is detection only; 1.1 fps is the full pipeline that actually measures speed. Quoting only 4.0 would describe a system that does not do the job.
  • The gates were written first and none was rewritten. Three of eight are recorded as failed. Lowering a criterion after seeing the measurement would have made all eight meaningless.
  • This measures speed; it is not a speed-enforcement device. A legally admissible speed measurement device requires type approval and periodic calibration certification against traceable standards, and this project has neither. Vehicles recorded above a configured limit are labelled “flagged for review”, never “violation”. No number plate or face is read, stored or recognised anywhere in the design.
